# Inference from Aggregated A/B-Test Data: Binary and Continuous Metrics

## Scope and assumptions

This report addresses fixed-horizon, two-sided frequentist inference for one control and one to three independently randomized variations, with one primary metric and many secondary metrics, including overlapping segment cells. It does not cover sequential inference, online FDR, bandits, covariate adjustment, or Bayesian decision rules.

I make the following assumptions. Binary metrics are generated by independent randomized units unless a metric is explicitly visit-, send-, order-, or event-level and the same randomized unit can contribute repeatedly. "Relative difference" means \(p_T/p_C-1\). The null for the reported p-value is ordinarily \(H_0:p_T=p_C\). Segment reports reuse units, so tests across metrics and cells are dependent. A "count, sum and sum of squares" is assumed to refer to the analysis-unit values unless stated otherwise. For ratio metrics, numerator and denominator totals may both vary by randomized unit. Multiplicity refers to a family defined within one completed experiment/report, not across experiments.

The literature also makes an important distinction between an interval being valid for its own inverted test and merely accompanying a different test's p-value. Koopman's score interval was expressly constructed to be consistent with Pearson's chi-squared test, while Miettinen-Nurminen constructs testing and interval estimation from the same constrained score function. Katz/log-Wald intervals instead correspond to Wald-type inference on the log risk ratio, not to the usual pooled two-proportion z-test.  [[turn862016search1](<https://doi.org/10.2307/2531405>); [turn862016search2](<https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.4780040211>)]

## Binary relative differences and confidence intervals

### Plain-language answer

For \(p_T/p_C-1\), all of the commonly used intervals become similar when both event counts are large and the control proportion is comfortably separated from zero. Their differences matter most precisely where an experimentation system is most fragile: rare events, zeros, highly unequal arm sizes, and large relative effects.

The delta interval on the **untransformed ratio** is a first-order normal approximation to \(\hat R=\hat p_T/\hat p_C\). It is symmetric around \(\hat R\), can produce a negative lower bound even though a risk ratio cannot be negative, and becomes unstable as the denominator approaches zero. A delta interval on \(\log R\) avoids negative endpoints and, with plug-in binomial variances, is essentially the classical Katz log interval. These are large-sample Wald constructions. Fagerland, Lydersen and Laake found that the Katz interval often had coverage farther from nominal than competing intervals, although its apparent conservatism depends on how zero-count cases are handled.  [[turn606024view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>); [turn383143view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>)]

For positive event counts, the Katz standard error is
\[
SE\{\log(\hat R)\}
=\sqrt{\frac1{x_T}-\frac1{n_T}+\frac1{x_C}-\frac1{n_C}},
\]
so the interval is
\[
\exp\left[\log(\hat R)\pm z_{\alpha/2}SE\{\log(\hat R)\}\right].
\]
Subtracting 1 from both endpoints gives the interval for relative difference. The construction is undefined if either event count is zero unless an artificial continuity correction or alternative estimator is introduced. Fagerland et al. explicitly distinguish Katz, adjusted-log, inverse-sinh and score methods in their coverage calculations.  [[turn606024view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>); [turn568817view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>)]

The **Koopman score interval** is obtained by inverting a score/Pearson statistic while estimating nuisance parameters under the proposed risk ratio. It is test-compatible with Pearson's chi-squared test at \(R=1\). Coverage studies repeatedly find it closer to nominal than Katz in many small and moderate samples, but it is not uniformly conservative: unequal sample sizes combined with small probabilities can produce undercoverage. It also has sensible boundary behavior in the score formulation, with a zero numerator allowing a lower endpoint of zero and a zero denominator allowing an infinite upper endpoint.  [[turn862016search1](<https://doi.org/10.2307/2531405>); [turn568817view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>); [turn606024view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>)]

The **Miettinen-Nurminen interval** uses restricted maximum-likelihood nuisance estimates in a score statistic for the hypothesized risk ratio. Its central advantage is the same one as Koopman's: testing and interval estimation use the null-constrained variance rather than the unrestricted plug-in variance. Simulation work by Nurminen reported coverage close to nominal for the constrained procedures, and Fagerland et al. describe the MN and Koopman versions as almost identical in this application. A 2024 re-analysis argues that the original MN finite-sample scaling factor is not exactly correct for every parameter value, although MN-type statistics still performed well relative to several alternatives in that study.  [[turn661933search2](<https://doi.org/10.1002/bimj.4710320210>); [turn568817view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>); `turn554464search4`]

**Fieller inference** treats a ratio by inverting a test on numerator minus \(R\) times denominator. Under an exact bivariate-normal model it can have exact coverage and, critically, is allowed to return an unbounded or disconnected confidence set when the denominator is statistically compatible with zero. That seemingly awkward behavior is a mathematical feature of ratio inference rather than merely a numerical failure. For independent binomial proportions, Fieller is itself approximate because the two sample proportions are only asymptotically normal. Deng, Knoblich and Lu's Bernoulli simulations found Fieller slightly closer to 95% than the simple delta interval at their smallest simulated sample size, with differences vanishing as sample sizes increased.  [[turn645624search1](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4796759/>); [turn507044view1](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>)]

For the **bootstrap**, the distinctions among percentile, BCa and studentized intervals matter less than the more fundamental singularity at a zero denominator. Generic bootstrap theory gives BCa and bootstrap-t higher-order accuracy than ordinary first-order normal or percentile intervals in smooth regular problems, but a binomial risk ratio near a boundary is not a smooth regular problem. In fact, a published result shows that ordinary bootstrap percentile intervals for a binomial proportion, and extensions to relative risk, can have infimum coverage zero for every fixed sample size. Specialized smooth-bootstrap work for relative risk has produced better average simulation performance in some configurations, but that result does not establish uniformly reliable coverage at the boundary.  [[turn554464search1](<https://doi.org/10.1214/ss/1032280214>); [turn661933search6](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spl.2013.09.005>); [turn661933search5](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4796759/>)]

With only binary counts, ordinary nonparametric bootstrap resampling is still computationally possible because a Bernoulli sample is completely determined by its number of zeroes and ones. BCa acceleration can likewise be reconstructed from the two possible leave-one-out cases. The practical complication is that bootstrap replicates can generate zero denominators, at which point percentile, BCa and studentized risk-ratio statistics need an explicit boundary convention. The generic bootstrap accuracy claims therefore should not be transferred automatically to rare-event risk ratios.  [[turn554464search1](<https://doi.org/10.1214/ss/1032280214>); [turn661933search6](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.spl.2013.09.005>)]

**Confidence: high** for the relative ordering of the main asymptotic methods and for test-interval compatibility; **medium** for bootstrap comparisons, because the literature directly comparing percentile, BCa and bootstrap-t specifically for independent-binomial risk ratios is much thinner than the literature on score and log intervals. Confidence would change materially with simulation results targeted to the report's actual arm sizes, baseline rates and zero-count frequency, especially if they compared all bootstrap variants under one common boundary rule.

## Low counts, zero counts and exact tests

### Plain-language answer

The familiar rule that a pooled two-proportion z-test is "valid when expected counts are at least 5" is a rule of thumb, not a coverage or Type I error theorem. The squared pooled z-statistic is Pearson's 1-df chi-squared statistic for the two-binomial equality null. Lydersen, Fagerland and Laake summarize Cochran's criterion as regarding Pearson's asymptotic test as inaccurate when any expected cell count is below 5, but other work shows that the required threshold is not constant and can be substantially above 5 depending on skewness and sample configuration. Consequently, "5, 10, 20 expected events" should be interpreted as progressively better asymptotic regimes, not three sharp validity boundaries.  [[turn606024view4](<https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.3531>); [turn572178search2](<https://doi.org/10.1080/02664760050120506>)]

At roughly **5 expected events per arm**, discreteness and skewness can be material, particularly if allocations are unequal. At roughly **10**, asymptotics are often appreciably better but still have no universal size guarantee. At **20**, the normal approximation is ordinarily much less stressed, but strong imbalance or rates extremely close to zero can still matter. The literature does not support a universal claim such as "20 expected events guarantees a 5% test"; the operating characteristic is a function of both sample sizes and the common null rate. This conclusion is also consistent with Brown, Cai and DasGupta's demonstration that textbook 5-or-10 rules for the one-binomial Wald interval can be unreliable even outside obvious boundary cases.  [[turn824938view0](<https://doi.org/10.1214/ss/1009213286>); [turn572178search2](<https://doi.org/10.1080/02664760050120506>)]

The **Wald family of intervals** has a more serious small-count problem than the pooled score test. For a single proportion, Brown, Cai and DasGupta document erratic coverage even at moderately large \(n\); for a risk ratio, Fagerland et al. find the Katz log-Wald interval materially inferior to score alternatives across many configurations. The relevant distinction is that the equality test uses a null-pooled variance, whereas a Wald interval typically uses unrestricted plug-in variances.  [[turn824938view0](<https://doi.org/10.1214/ss/1009213286>); [turn606024view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>)]

For exact testing, **Fisher's exact test** conditions on the observed success total. This guarantees size control under its conditional construction, but the discreteness makes it conservative when the column total was not fixed by design. **Boschloo's test** uses Fisher's p-value as the ordering statistic in an unconditional exact test and is uniformly more powerful than Fisher's conditional test in the comparisons covered by the cited literature. "Barnard's test" is less uniquely defined in current software because several unconditional orderings are used under that label; unconditional tests based on an efficient or pooled score statistic can outperform Fisher, while poorly chosen unconditional statistics can actually be less powerful.  [[turn921511search8](<https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9574.1970.tb00104.x>); [turn606024view4](<https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.3531>); [turn706579search5](<https://doi.org/10.1111/1541-0420.00051>)]

A **mid-p** value removes half the probability mass of the observed table from an ordinary exact tail. It is typically much less conservative and often close to unconditional exact results, but it is no longer exact in the strict size-control sense and can slightly exceed nominal Type I error for some parameter values. Hirji, Tan and Elashoff found mid-p actual levels closer to nominal than several classical alternatives over a wide set of sample sizes; Lydersen et al. report the same tradeoff.  [[turn921511search2](<https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.4780100713>); [turn606024view4](<https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.3531>)]

Zeros need to be separated into different cases. If treatment has zero events and control has positive events, \(\hat R=0\) and relative difference is \(-1\), although the log estimator is undefined. If control has zero and treatment has positive events, the empirical ratio is \(+\infty\), so a finite percent lift is not defined. If both arms have zero events, the empirical ratio is \(0/0\) and has no numerical meaning. Equality of proportions can nevertheless still be tested when only one arm is zero because the pooled null variance is positive. When both event counts are zero, the usual pooled z statistic is \(0/0\); exact tests carry no evidence against equality and return a non-significant result. Score or exact risk-ratio confidence sets naturally admit boundary endpoints such as zero or infinity, whereas log-Wald formulas require a correction to produce any answer.  [[turn568817view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>); `turn706579search9`]

For a reporting system, this creates a distinction between the **effect-scale result** and the **equality test**: the RR-based relative difference can be undefined or infinite while an absolute risk difference and a test of equality remain mathematically defined. Reporting counts and arm rates therefore carries information that cannot always be represented by a single percent-lift number.  [[turn706579search1](<https://doi.org/10.1214/21-SS131>); [turn568817view0](<https://doi.org/10.1177/0962280211415469>)]

Multiplicity should be attached to a pre-defined family of hypotheses, not merely to the set of comparisons that happened to produce finite-looking statistics. Under ordinary Bonferroni or Holm logic, a hypothesis that cannot produce a useful rejection can conceptually be given \(p=1\), so it remains in the family and only adds conservatism. In discrete problems, however, specialized procedures can validly exploit each test's **minimum attainable p-value** and thereby remove hypotheses that cannot possibly cross the current threshold. Tarone's modified Bonferroni procedure and later discrete stepwise methods formalize that distinction.  [[turn454888search1](<https://doi.org/10.2307/2531456>); [turn454888search7](<https://doi.org/10.1080/19466315.2019.1654912>)]

This matters strongly in the stated reporting system because many binary outcomes are sparse. Ordinary Bonferroni/Holm ignores both discreteness and the strong dependence induced by overlapping metrics and segments, so it can be conservative. Westfall and Troendle show that discreteness alone can dramatically reduce the effective burden for sparse binary outcomes while preserving FWER, and joint resampling can additionally exploit dependence. The latter requires joint randomized-unit data or sufficient cross-metric covariance summaries; independent per-cell counts cannot reconstruct that dependence.  [[turn454888search3](<https://doi.org/10.1002/bimj.200710456>); `turn454888search4`]

**Confidence: high** that 5, 10 and 20 should not be treated as universal validity cutoffs, and high on the Fisher/Boschloo/mid-p tradeoffs. **Medium** on statements about the numerical error rate at exactly 5, 10 or 20 expected events because no single simulation design can represent the wide range of sample-size imbalance and null rates in this system. Confidence would change with a design-specific enumeration of Type I error over the actual arm-size and baseline-rate grid.

## Continuous, ratio and clustered metrics from aggregates

### Plain-language answer

If each arm's \(n\), sum and sum of squares are computed over **independent randomized units**, the ordinary sample mean, unbiased sample variance, Welch mean-difference statistic and Welch confidence interval can all be reconstructed exactly, apart from floating-point rounding. No unit-level records are required for those calculations. The key statistical assumption is therefore not normal raw revenue, but that the per-unit observations are independent across randomized units and have sufficiently well-behaved finite moments for the sample-mean approximation at the available sample size. Deng et al. explicitly note that mean and variance can be represented by low-dimensional summary statistics in distributed systems.  [[turn954169view1](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>)]

Highly skewed, non-negative, zero-inflated revenue does not by itself invalidate inference on a mean. The central limit theorem applies to sample means under finite variance, but convergence can be slow when the tail is extreme. Balanced randomized groups have a useful robustness property because under the null the difference of two equally sized samples from the same population is much more symmetric than either sample mean alone. Online-experiment simulations reported by Kohavi, Deng and Vermeer emphasize this benefit of equal allocation; newer general simulations nevertheless show that Welch can have inflated Type I error when strong skewness is combined with unequal sample sizes.  [[turn907861search8](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3534678.3539160>); [turn954764search6](<https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-026-02909-5>)]

Thus, for a zero-inflated revenue metric, the important quantities are the number of independent randomized units and tail severity, not simply the percentage of zeros. A million users with a finite but skewed per-user revenue distribution can be a much better Welch regime than a few dozen users even when both have the same proportion of zero spenders. Conversely, treating millions of visits as independent when only thousands of customers were randomized creates a falsely large effective sample size.  [[turn907861search9](<https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2023.2257237>); [turn507044view2](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>)]

For a **ratio metric**
\[
R=\frac{\sum_i Y_i}{\sum_i X_i}
=\frac{\bar Y}{\bar X},
\]
the delta-method variance depends on the variance of \(Y_i\), the variance of \(X_i\), and their covariance. Deng, Knoblich and Lu derive
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\hat R)
\approx
\frac1n\left[
\frac{s_Y^2}{\bar X^2}
-2\frac{\bar Y}{\bar X^3}s_{XY}
+\frac{\bar Y^2}{\bar X^4}s_X^2
\right].
\]
Consequently, the aggregate sufficient statistics for this first-order calculation are \(n,\sum X,\sum Y,\sum X^2,\sum Y^2,\sum XY\). A count, numerator sum and denominator sum alone are not enough because they omit the covariance term.  [[turn507044view1](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>)]

That framework covers metrics such as revenue per order, clicks per visit, or revenue per active customer when numerator and denominator are first accumulated **per randomized unit**. If customers are randomized and customer \(i\) has total revenue \(S_i\) and order count \(N_i\), average order value \(\sum_i S_i/\sum_iN_i\) is a ratio of customer-level totals. Its variance needs the between-customer variance of \(S_i\), variance of \(N_i\), and covariance \(S_i,N_i\). The same logic applies to visits or sends.  [[turn507044view2](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>)]

Repeated observations create the most consequential aggregate-data limitation. If one customer contributes several visits or orders, storing only event-level \(n,\sum y,\sum y^2\) loses the within-customer covariance needed for correct variance estimation. For a ratio metric under customer randomization, the required customer-level summaries can be reduced to \(K,\sum S_i,\sum N_i,\sum S_i^2,\sum N_i^2,\sum S_iN_i\). Deng et al. derive the clustered ratio variance by treating each cluster's total outcome and size as the two random variables in the delta method.  [[turn507044view2](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>)]

For a simple per-customer mean, aggregation is easier: first collapse all activity to one value \(Y_i\) per randomized customer, then retain \(K,\sum_iY_i,\sum_iY_i^2\). If the system instead retains only global visit-level moments, no algebra can recover the missing customer clustering exactly. This is an information-loss problem, not a choice of standard-error formula.  [[turn507044view2](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>)]

### Numerically stable variance and merge formulas

In exact arithmetic,
\[
s^2=\frac{Q-S^2/n}{n-1},
\quad S=\sum x_i,\quad Q=\sum x_i^2.
\]
The summaries \(n,S,Q\) are mergeable across disjoint dates or segment partitions by addition. Numerically, however, \(Q-S^2/n\) can suffer catastrophic cancellation when the mean is large relative to the standard deviation. Chan, Golub and LeVeque show that the textbook one-pass form can even produce a negative corrected sum of squares because two large nearly equal numbers are subtracted.  [[turn954169view2](<https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.1983.10483115>); [turn568817view1](<https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.1983.10483115>)]

A stable merge representation is \((n,\mu,M_2)\), where \(M_2=\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\). For blocks \(A,B\),
\[
\delta=\mu_B-\mu_A,\quad
n=n_A+n_B,
\]
\[
\mu=\mu_A+\delta\frac{n_B}{n},
\]
\[
M_2=M_{2A}+M_{2B}+\delta^2\frac{n_An_B}{n}.
\]
Pairwise centered-moment updates are specifically designed for distributed and streaming computation and avoid the cancellation inherent in raw power sums.  [[turn963310search2](<https://doi.org/10.2172/1028931>); [turn963310search5](<https://doi.org/10.2172/1028931>)]

If old data already exist only as rounded \(S\) and \(Q\), converting them afterward to \(M_2\) cannot recover digits already lost by cancellation. The stable representation therefore matters at the stage where summaries are first produced or merged.  [[turn568817view1](<https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.1983.10483115>); [turn963310search2](<https://doi.org/10.2172/1028931>)]

### Capping and winsorising

Capping or winsorising changes the estimand. A fixed cap applied identically to every unit turns \(Y\) into \(g(Y)=\min(Y,c)\), after which ordinary randomization-based or large-sample mean inference concerns the mean of that **capped** outcome, not raw revenue. Online-experiment literature documents capping as a variance-reduction technique for highly skewed metrics, while also noting that the resulting metric is intentionally different from the uncapped business total.  [[turn551586search1](<https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2023.2257237>); `turn551586search10`]

A data-dependent percentile cap creates additional inferential issues because the threshold itself is random. A recent RCT study comparing pooled and arm-specific winsorisation found that the choice changes both treatment-effect bias and Type I/II error, with arm-specific winsorisation reducing one form of bias but increasing Type I error in its simulations. This is evidence against treating winsorisation as an innocuous numerical cleanup step.  [[turn551586search4](<https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jdeveco.2026.103815>)]

With only \(n,\sum x,\sum x^2\), retrospective capping or winsorising cannot generally be reconstructed because one needs to know which observations exceeded the threshold and by how much. It therefore has to occur before those moments are discarded, or the aggregation layer must retain richer tail information. That is a mathematical consequence of the insufficiency of the three moments for nonlinear tail transformations.  [[turn963310search2](<https://doi.org/10.2172/1028931>)]

**Confidence: high** on which aggregates are sufficient for Welch and first-order delta inference, and high that event-level moments are insufficient for clustered randomized-unit inference. **Medium** on finite-sample Welch behavior for any particular revenue distribution because tail behavior is application-specific. Confidence would change with A/A re-randomization or simulation using the actual per-randomized-unit revenue distribution and the actual treatment/control allocation ratio.

## Cross-cutting tradeoffs for the reporting architecture

The binary and continuous problems share one architectural theme: summary data are adequate only when they preserve the variance structure at the **randomization unit**. A binary visit count is sufficient for a binomial model only if visits are independent experimental units; a revenue sum and sum of squares are sufficient for Welch only if the values being squared are independent analysis units; a ratio needs the numerator-denominator cross-product; repeated activity needs cluster-level second moments.  [[turn954169view1](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>); [turn507044view2](<https://doi.org/10.1145/3219819.3219919>)]

The large number of overlapping segment cells creates a second theme. Marginal p-values and intervals for each cell can be computed from cell-level summaries, but dependence-aware multiplicity procedures cannot generally be reconstructed from those summaries because the same user's membership across metrics and segments determines cross-test covariance. Bonferroni-type methods do not need that covariance, while resampling or correlation-aware procedures do. This is why an aggregated reporting layer can preserve valid marginal inference yet permanently discard information needed for less conservative joint inference.  [`turn454888search0`; [turn454888search3](<https://doi.org/10.1002/bimj.200710456>)]

A third theme is test-interval coherence. The percent-lift interval and the displayed p-value can answer the same null with different approximations. Katz/log-delta plus a pooled z p-value is such a mixed pair. Koopman plus Pearson, or an MN score interval plus its matching constrained score test, is much closer to literal inversion consistency. Exact unconditional methods can also be constructed with matching confidence intervals, but Fay and Hunsberger emphasize that no non-asymptotic method simultaneously optimizes validity, power, compatibility, coherence and all other desirable properties.  [[turn862016search1](<https://doi.org/10.2307/2531405>); [turn706579search1](<https://doi.org/10.1214/21-SS131>)]

Finally, the strongest disagreement in the literature concerns what "exact" should mean operationally. Fisher's conditional method offers strict size control but sacrifices power through conditioning and discreteness; unconditional exact methods preserve size without conditioning but depend on an ordering statistic and nuisance-parameter maximization; mid-p sacrifices guaranteed size control to reduce discreteness-induced conservatism. These are genuine inferential tradeoffs rather than merely implementation details.  [[turn606024view4](<https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.3531>); [turn706579search5](<https://doi.org/10.1111/1541-0420.00051>)]
