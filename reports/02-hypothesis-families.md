# Defining hypothesis families for multiplicity control in an A/B-test reporting system

## Assumptions and scope

I assume each randomized test is analyzed once at a prespecified fixed horizon, with two-sided frequentist tests, no interim looks, no adaptive stopping, and no reuse of the same test for later confirmatory decisions. I treat each variation-versus-control contrast on each metric and reporting cell as a distinct elementary hypothesis unless a testing procedure explicitly combines them. I also assume that "family" means the collection of hypotheses over which a specified error criterion, usually strong familywise error rate (FWER), is intended to hold. FDA defines its principal multiplicity concern in terms of false favorable conclusions across planned primary and secondary hypotheses, and EMA similarly uses strong FWER, meaning control of the probability of rejecting at least one true null regardless of which nulls are true. I do not assume that a common denominator, common metric, common segment, common protocol, or common control group mechanically determines family membership. The literature is explicit that the word "family" does not have a universally fixed statistical definition. A practically important view is to group hypotheses that create multiple opportunities for the same scientific or decision claim, while another, stricter regulatory view often treats all confirmatory claims in the same study as subject to study-wise strong FWER control. Howard et al. explicitly document this disagreement. The conclusions below are about error-rate definitions and trade-offs, not a recommendation for which policy the reporting system should adopt.

## Defining the family

**Plain-language answer. Confidence: high.** A defensible family is not determined by where results happen to appear in the report. It is determined by what collection of tests creates interchangeable opportunities to make the claim you wish to protect against false positives. If any one of three variations can be called a winner on the primary metric, the three variation-versus-control hypotheses naturally form one family for that claim. If any one of 12 metrics can establish "the treatment worked", those hypotheses likewise create multiplicity for that claim. Splitting them into separate "per metric", "per segment", or "per denominator" families does not remove the multiplicity if the ultimate decision selects the favorable result across those partitions. EMA states the central principle in terms of opportunity for favorable selection, while FDA describes endpoint families according to the role endpoints play in establishing or extending efficacy claims. What would change the confidence: a materially different decision rule. If each test genuinely supports independent, separately interpretable claims, with no selection of one hypothesis to stand in for another, then a broader family is less compelling. Howard et al. argue precisely this for genuinely distinct treatments, while EMA takes a more conservative confirmatory-study position. ### Family boundaries and shared controls

A shared control does **not by itself** create a new multiplicity problem. It makes the treatment-versus-control statistics correlated. Howard et al. show that positive correlation from a shared control can actually make the FWER for unadjusted tests smaller than it would be for the equivalent independent comparisons, although it increases the probability of multiple false positives occurring together. Their conclusion is that adjustment should not be justified merely because data from the same control arm are reused. The stronger argument for grouping several variations into one family is instead the decision structure. If the system asks "is any variation better than control?" or chooses the best-looking variation, multiple hypotheses contribute to one success opportunity. EMA's 2002 guidance says multiplicity matters where there is an opportunity to choose the most favorable result from two or more analyses. FDA makes the analogous point for multiple primary endpoints where each one independently provides a route to study success. For one to three variations compared with the same control, Dunnett's procedure is tailored to exactly the many-to-one setting. It uses the joint distribution of treatment-versus-control statistics and therefore exploits their correlation. Bonferroni instead controls FWER using only the union bound, and Holm is a sequentially rejective Bonferroni procedure that remains valid under arbitrary dependence. Dunnett can therefore be less conservative than Bonferroni or Holm when its joint-distribution assumptions are appropriate, but it is specialized to comparisons with a common control rather than an arbitrary collection of hypotheses. Dunnett's original 1955 paper introduced the procedure specifically for simultaneous comparisons of several treatments against one control; a later Dunnett paper explicitly describes the procedure as controlling the experimentwise error rate. For selecting the best of several variations, ordinary unadjusted estimation has a second problem beyond hypothesis-test FWER: the selected winner's effect estimate is upward biased because selection favors positive noise. Recent work on "inference on winners" shows that even unbiased randomized-trial estimates suffer this winner's curse after data-driven selection. Hsu's multiple-comparisons-with-the-best framework is one classical simultaneous-inference formulation for identifying treatments that cannot be distinguished from the best. A denominator such as "per send" versus "per click among clickers" changes the estimand and potentially the analyzed population, but it is not by itself an error-rate principle. If the report permits the analyst or reader to choose whichever denominator produces significance as evidence for the same substantive claim, both hypotheses belong to the relevant claim-level family. If they answer genuinely different, separately labeled questions and neither substitutes for the other, they can reasonably be treated as separate inferential families. This follows from the claim-based conception of family in the methodological debate, rather than from any theorem that denominators define families. ### Key claims and passages

## Primary and secondary metrics

**Plain-language answer. Confidence: high.** A single prespecified primary hypothesis can be tested at the full nominal alpha when it is truly the only hypothesis that determines primary success. But if there are several variations and success on **any** variation-versus-control primary test can establish success, there are several primary hypotheses and treating every one at full alpha does not control the FWER for the primary decision. FDA's guidance makes the distinction explicitly: one prespecified primary endpoint has no multiple-endpoint multiplicity, whereas several primary endpoints, any one of which suffices for efficacy, require multiplicity control. EMA similarly says the simplest no-adjustment case is two groups, one primary variable, one prespecified primary null, and no interim analysis. What would change the confidence: if "primary metric" is only a reporting label and not a privileged decision hypothesis, or if each variation is treated as an independent scientific claim rather than alternative routes to the same test-level conclusion. The latter is the main area in which the literature contains a principled disagreement. ### Arguments for and against leaving the primary uncorrected

The argument **for** full-alpha testing is strongest when there is exactly one prespecified primary hypothesis. It also applies to intersection-union situations where every primary criterion must succeed, because there is no opportunity to choose among them. EMA says that when statistical significance is required on all primary variables, no alpha splitting is needed. FDA says the same where study success depends on all primary endpoints. The argument **against** uncorrected testing becomes stronger whenever there are several routes to primary success: several variants, several primary outcomes, several populations, or combinations of these. FDA calls these "multiple chances of success". EMA characterizes the concern as the possibility of favorable selection. For secondary metrics, there is a sharp distinction between exploratory and confirmatory use. EMA says secondary endpoints intended only as supportive evidence may be analyzed exploratorily, with no claim attached. Secondary variables intended to support additional confirmatory claims must enter a confirmatory testing strategy after the primary objective has been established. FDA likewise says exploratory endpoints generally do not require multiplicity adjustment because they are not used to support conclusions, but secondary endpoints capable of supporting additional effects should be included in the type-I-error control plan. ### Fixed sequence, fallback, gatekeeping and alpha recycling

A **fixed-sequence** procedure orders hypotheses in advance, tests the first at full alpha, and advances only after rejection. FDA states that the order must be prospectively specified and that confirmatory testing stops at the first failure. It controls the FWER while allowing each reached hypothesis to use full alpha. Its power cost is highly asymmetric: there is little or no multiplicity penalty for early hypotheses, but a failed early hypothesis makes the confirmatory power for all later hypotheses effectively zero. A **fallback** procedure assigns nonzero alpha to several hypotheses initially, orders them in advance, and transfers alpha forward when earlier hypotheses reject. Wiens and Dmitrienko show that it controls FWER, allows later hypotheses to remain testable even when an earlier one fails, and generally trades some power away from the first hypothesis to protect power later in the sequence. Compared with fixed sequence, it is less brittle; compared with plain Bonferroni, successful early tests can increase the alpha available downstream. **Gatekeeping** operates on families rather than a single list. In **serial gatekeeping**, the next family opens only when the specified criterion in the previous family has been met, for example all co-primary hypotheses rejecting. In **parallel gatekeeping**, rejection of one or more hypotheses in the earlier family can release alpha to the next family. FDA describes both structures and says the logical relationships must be specified. Dmitrienko, Offen and Westfall's 2003 paper developed gatekeeping methods specifically for primary, secondary and tertiary families, under closed testing. **Alpha recycling** is the more general idea that alpha attached to a rejected hypothesis can be transferred to other hypotheses according to prespecified weights. Bretz et al.'s graphical framework represents hypotheses as nodes and transfer rules as directed weighted edges. The approach is a representation of sequentially rejective, weighted Bonferroni-type procedures, not permission to redistribute alpha after looking at results. The key error rate for all of the standard fixed-sequence, fallback, closed gatekeeping, and Bretz-style graphical procedures discussed here is **strong FWER**, not FDR. The details of the procedure determine whether this control is guaranteed under arbitrary dependence or requires specific local tests or assumptions. The 2008 multistage gatekeeping paper notes, for example, that ordinary Bonferroni is separable while standard Holm, Hochberg, fallback, and Dunnett are not automatically separable for that gatekeeping construction; truncated variants are used to recover the needed property. The power trade-off for secondary metrics is therefore structural rather than a single universal percentage. A hard gate gives secondary metrics maximal available alpha once the gate opens but zero confirmatory opportunity if it does not. Fallback reserves some alpha for lower-priority hypotheses and thus sacrifices some power upstream to retain downstream opportunity. Parallel gatekeeping and graphical recycling can distribute alpha more flexibly, at the cost of more design choices that must be made before seeing the data. FDA warns more generally that as the number of secondary endpoints grows, multiplicity adjustment can make individual secondary findings increasingly hard to establish, while a long hierarchy may leave low-ranked hypotheses untested. ### Key claims and passages

## Drill-downs by segment

**Plain-language answer. Confidence: high for the statistical principles, medium for mapping them to very large overlapping A/B-report cell systems.** Segment drill-downs can be treated either as exploratory descriptions or as confirmatory hypotheses. If they are confirmatory and the system is willing to claim a treatment effect because any one of many segments is significant, they create multiplicity and belong in an error-control strategy. If they are exploratory, the report can show estimates and uncertainty without giving them the same inferential status as prespecified confirmatory conclusions. EMA's subgroup guidance explicitly separates confirmatory subgroup testing from exploratory subgroup investigation. What would change the confidence: a formal hierarchical structure for the segments, such as country then region then city, with mutually nested child cells and a prespecified traversal rule. That structure enables tree-specific methods. In the setting described, however, results for every segment value and every combination of segment values create many overlapping cells. Those cells generally do **not** form a simple tree because one observation can belong simultaneously to many non-nested cells, for example age 18-34, mobile, US, and mobile-US. Tree procedures require a genuine hierarchy of disjoint or nested subfamilies, not merely a display that looks hierarchical. Yekutieli's hierarchical FDR work explicitly assumes hypotheses arranged in a tree of disjoint subfamilies. ### Overall-then-subgroup testing

Testing an overall population first and allowing subgroup testing only after an overall success can reduce the number of confirmatory paths, but the validity of a specific hierarchical procedure depends on the exact logical gate and local multiple-testing method. It does **not** automatically license arbitrary post hoc subgroup searching. EMA's 2019 subgroup guidance says a subgroup included in the confirmatory strategy is fundamentally a multiple-testing problem because hypotheses may concern both the subgroup and the full population. For a true tree, hierarchical testing can be efficient because parents can screen entire branches. Yekutieli's 2008 paper is an FDR method rather than an FWER method, but it is important here because it makes the structural assumption explicit: the tested hypotheses are arranged in a tree of disjoint subfamilies. It therefore does not directly justify treating arbitrary overlapping cross-classifications as a tree. Overlapping, non-tree cells should normally be counted as separate elementary hypotheses if each cell can independently generate a claim. Dependence among their test statistics matters for how conservative or powerful a correction is, but dependence does not make hypotheses disappear from the family. Bonferroni and Holm remain valid without needing independence assumptions, while procedures exploiting dependence can gain power when their assumptions are justified. The regulatory guidance repeatedly treats multiple populations or subgroups as another source of multiplicity rather than as something eliminated by overlap. ### Selection, exaggeration, sign errors and shrinkage

Selecting only significant or extreme segment effects creates a winner's-curse problem even if multiplicity-adjusted testing correctly controls false rejections. Selection changes the conditional distribution of the reported estimates. The selected estimates tend to be too far from zero, particularly when power is low or the number of candidates is large. Andrews, Kitagawa and McCloskey give a modern formal treatment of this selection-induced bias, while Gelman and Carlin frame related phenomena as Type M magnitude exaggeration and Type S sign errors. Multiplicity correction and shrinkage therefore solve different problems. FWER correction addresses the probability of false rejection. Shrinkage or selection-adjusted inference addresses effect-size overstatement after selecting extreme estimates. A result can pass a valid familywise test and still have a selected point estimate that is exaggerated. The empirical RCT literature likewise finds substantial magnitude exaggeration when true effects are modest and evidence is noisy. Selective-family inference provides another warning. Benjamini, Bogomolov and related selective-inference work shows that controlling error separately inside each family does not necessarily control error among families that were themselves selected because they looked promising. The JRSSB paper on selective inference states this directly. ### Interaction tests versus separate subgroup tests

A treatment-by-segment interaction asks whether the treatment effect **differs across segment levels**. A treatment test within one subgroup asks whether the treatment effect differs from zero **inside that subgroup**. Those are not the same null hypothesis. A significant effect in one subgroup and a nonsignificant effect in another does not establish heterogeneity. Cochrane guidance and subgroup-methodology reviews explicitly warn against making that inference. An interaction test controls the type I error for the hypothesis of effect homogeneity for that particular interaction, subject to the model assumptions and whatever multiplicity adjustment applies across multiple interactions. It does not by itself control the FWER for subsequent searches across every individual segment cell, nor does it establish a nonzero treatment effect in a specific subgroup. Conversely, subgroup-specific treatment tests can establish within-cell effects if properly multiplicity-adjusted, but they do not test whether those effects differ from neighboring cells. Interaction tests are also often weakly powered. A review in *Statistics in Medicine* notes that trials sized for the overall treatment effect frequently have limited power for treatment-by-subgroup interactions. Thus failure to reject an interaction does not establish homogeneity. EMA similarly treats subgroup credibility as depending not only on statistical evidence but also on prespecification, plausibility and replication. EMA's 2019 guidance gives an especially relevant caution for your reporting architecture: considering multiple subgroups increases false-positive opportunities, and using combinations of several factors exacerbates the problem further. It also notes that reviewers are naturally drawn to extreme subgroup estimates, exactly the selection mechanism that produces exaggerated observed effects. ### Key claims and passages

## Practical interpretation of the competing family definitions

The literature supports several coherent but different error-control targets. They should not be conflated.

A **per-comparison** policy keeps each elementary type I error at alpha but does not promise a bound on the probability of at least one false finding across the report. A **per-metric** family controls false positive risk across variations, segments, or both for one metric, but does not protect a decision that searches across metrics. A **per-segment** family has the analogous weakness if the user searches across segments. A **per-test or per-experiment** family controls all prespecified confirmatory opportunities inside the randomized test, and is closest to FDA and EMA study-wise confirmatory thinking. A **program-wide** family is stronger still, but neither the FDA multiple-endpoint guidance nor the EMA multiplicity document says that all independent experiments in an ongoing research program must be bundled into a single classical FWER family. FDA's guidance is explicitly focused on multiplicity within a single trial. The major unresolved issue is philosophical rather than mathematical: when are several hypotheses separate scientific claims versus alternative ways to earn the same favorable conclusion? Howard et al. argue that distinct experimental therapies need not be grouped merely because they share a protocol and control, whereas EMA's confirmatory-trial guidance emphasizes study-wise strong control and treats multiple dose comparisons as requiring FWER control when the objective is to identify an effective dose. These positions are not logically inconsistent because they define the protected claim differently, but they lead to different families in a reporting system. For the particular architecture in the question, the largest conceptual trap is defining families from database organization rather than decision opportunity. "Per denominator", "per segment", and "per metric" are technical partitions. They are defensible family boundaries only when they also correspond to genuinely separate claims. If users can scan across those partitions and elevate whichever cell is favorable into the same conclusion, the statistical family relevant to that conclusion spans the partitions. This is the common thread running through EMA's "opportunity to choose" formulation, FDA's "multiple chances of success" formulation, and Howard et al.'s "single claim of effectiveness" formulation. ## Source table
