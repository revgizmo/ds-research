# Multiplicity correction in A/B-test reporting systems

## Scope and assumptions

This report addresses the fixed-horizon, two-sided frequentist setting in the question. I treat a "family" as the set of hypotheses jointly entering the advertised multiplicity procedure. I distinguish FWER control from FDR control, and I distinguish corrections used for confirmatory experiment conclusions from exploratory segment or drill-down analyses. I also treat a metric-by-treatment comparison as a distinct hypothesis unless the platform documentation explicitly says otherwise.

The following assumptions were necessary because public documentation is incomplete or sometimes describes several product modes at once:

- "Current" means documentation accessible on November 10, 2026. Where a vendor has both legacy and newer products, I do not assume they use the same statistics.
- A statement such as "Bonferroni across metrics and treatments" is interpreted as FWER control over the stated family, unless the vendor expressly describes something else.
- For internal systems at LinkedIn, Microsoft, Booking.com, Netflix, and Airbnb, public papers and engineering posts describe practices at particular dates. I do not assume those practices are still the current company-wide default.
- "Segment correction" means multiplicity adjustment across subgroup estimates or subgroup hypotheses, not merely recomputing the top-line correction after filtering.
- "Intervals adjusted" means the displayed confidence interval is changed to correspond to the multiplicity-adjusted inference. I do not infer this merely because adjusted p-values are displayed.
- When documentation describes a statistical issue but does not say the product automatically corrects it, I classify the product behavior as "not stated", not as "no correction".
- Several vendors now emphasize sequential testing or Bayesian modes. Those features are outside the requested decision setting. I report multiplicity behavior only insofar as the documentation reveals the underlying correction, family, defaults, or UI controls.

The most important overall finding is that there is no industry consensus on a single family or error rate. Publicly documented systems span FDR control, FWER control, role-weighted FWER, informal prior-based thresholds, and no automatic correction for exploratory drill-downs. The largest recurring distinction is whether the family is defined by all metric-treatment pairs or only by metrics that can positively trigger a ship decision. ## Procedures and error rates

### Plain-language answer

**Confidence: high for commercial platforms with current documentation, medium for company-internal systems.** What would change the confidence most is access to current internal platform specifications for LinkedIn, Microsoft, Booking.com, Netflix, and Airbnb, and a current technical specification for VWO's fixed-horizon mode.

The documented commercial systems divide roughly into three designs.

First, **Optimizely uses FDR rather than FWER as the central multiplicity target** in Stats Engine. Its original technical paper explicitly says it replaced Type I error control with false-discovery-rate control and used the Benjamini-Hochberg construction. Current Optimizely documentation describes a tiered metric family: the primary metric is isolated from other metrics, secondary metrics are adjusted based on metrics and variations, and monitoring metrics receive fractional weight so that they minimally affect secondary metrics and do not affect the primary. Second, **Statsig, Eppo, GrowthBook, Amplitude, LaunchDarkly, and Spotify Confidence expose more conventional multiple-comparison procedures.** Statsig exposes Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg and lets the correction family be variants, metrics, or both. Eppo uses a preferential Bonferroni construction that allocates a designated share of alpha to the primary metric and the remainder over other metric-treatment comparisons. GrowthBook publicly describes Holm-Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg. Amplitude uses Bonferroni by default over treatments and primary/secondary metrics. LaunchDarkly exposes a correction scope across treatments, metrics, or both and its public API exposes a multiple-comparison-correction method field. Spotify Confidence currently describes Bonferroni over success metrics, with guardrails excluded from that positive-decision family. Third, **the large internal platforms documented in papers do not reveal one uniform correction regime.** LinkedIn's 2015 public practice was a two-threshold rule: roughly 0.05 for metrics expected to move and 0.001 for metrics not expected to move, explicitly motivated as a simple alternative to asking experimenters to manage thousands of multiple tests. Microsoft ExP publicly says that its alerting system uses Benjamini-Hochberg FDR adjustment, and in 2026 Microsoft described BH as the default for a scorecard-level "treatment effect assessment." Booking.com publicly emphasizes pre-specification and minimizing analyst degrees of freedom, but I found no current public source establishing a universal metric-by-metric correction procedure. Netflix states that correlated metrics and multiple treatment cells create a multiple-testing problem but does not identify a correction in the public material I could read. Airbnb papers emphasize winner's curse and post-selection bias from many metrics, segments, and treatments rather than documenting a current automatic family-wise correction in ERF. ### Platform-by-platform detail

### Plain-language answer

**Confidence: high for Statsig, Eppo, Amplitude, LaunchDarkly, and Adobe planning controls; medium for Optimizely, GrowthBook, and VWO; low for the internal company systems.** This would change with current product-admin screenshots or API specifications that enumerate defaults and mutability.

There are two sharply different philosophies in current products. Optimizely largely embeds its multiplicity policy into metric ranking and Stats Engine rather than making the user choose among several named corrections. Statsig, Eppo, GrowthBook, Amplitude, and LaunchDarkly expose substantially more control. Spotify Confidence exposes metric roles as part of the decision framework but presents Bonferroni as a deliberate platform choice rather than a menu of correction algorithms. **Statsig** has the broadest clearly documented menu among the sources reviewed. Organization admins can set project-wide defaults; individual experiments can change them. The experiment can apply Bonferroni per variant, Bonferroni per metric, BH per variant and metric, and BH only to primary metrics. For Bonferroni across metrics, "Primary Metric Weight" allocates a chosen share of alpha to primary metrics. The confidence level is separately selectable. The documentation does not reveal one universal factory default for multiplicity across all organizations. **Eppo** allows company-wide defaults and experiment-level overrides for analysis method, confidence level, desired power, and whether multiple-testing correction is enabled. The correction method itself is preferential Bonferroni rather than a menu of procedures, and the primary-metric alpha weight is configurable. The documentation explicitly gives 95% as the default confidence level, but the pages I read do not state that multiplicity correction itself is enabled by default. **Amplitude** enables Bonferroni by default and allows it to be toggled off. Confidence level is exposed in statistical preferences, with 95% shown as the standard/default setup. Amplitude's documentation gives an explicit reason to switch it off: compatibility with an internal system that does not use Bonferroni, provided the user accepts the higher false-positive rate. **LaunchDarkly** exposes whether MCC is applied, the scope across treatments, metrics, or both, significance level, and test direction. The API exposes a `multipleComparisonCorrectionMethod` and `multipleComparisonCorrectionScope`, although the documentation retrieved does not give a complete list of allowable correction methods in the same passage. Filtered subgroup results deliberately drop MCC regardless of the top-line setting. **GrowthBook** exposes multiple-testing correction as an organization-level default in current public material, and its 2023 version announcement explicitly said users could specify Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg. More recent GrowthBook material describes Holm-Bonferroni and BH rather than ordinary Bonferroni, suggesting a product evolution, but I did not locate a canonical release note pinning the exact change date from Bonferroni to Holm-Bonferroni. **Adobe Target** exposes significance/confidence inputs in its sample-size calculator and an optional "Apply correction" control for more than two offers. This is a planning control, not evidence that ordinary Target reporting automatically adjusts every displayed p-value or interval. Adobe's own A4T documentation explicitly says its confidence calculation does not apply Bonferroni for multiple offers. **Optimizely** allows the project-level statistical significance threshold to be changed, but the current documentation describes its metric-role hierarchy as built into Stats Engine. The 2015 technical paper says the FDR threshold was a project-level setting. The current material I read does not present a user-selectable choice between FDR, Bonferroni, Holm, and no correction. **VWO** currently markets extensive statistical configuration and a built-in Bonferroni correction for multiple variations, but the public pages retrieved do not state whether the multiple-variation correction can be disabled independently in fixed-horizon mode, nor do they document a user-selectable correction family across metrics. An older/current multivariate-testing page says "latest Sidak correction" while the current statistics page says "built-in Bonferroni correction." That may represent different product paths rather than a dated method replacement, so I cannot assign a reliable change date. The public internal-company sources mostly describe statistical policy rather than UI configuration. LinkedIn's 0.05/0.001 thresholds were a rule of thumb for experimenters. Booking.com's public guidance instead argues for reducing analyst degrees of freedom and pre-specifying processing choices. Microsoft's 2026 ExP article says BH-adjusted scorecard p-values are now reported by default for its treatment-effect assessment, but does not say this is a user-selectable correction. ## Reasons, limitations, and changes over time

### Plain-language answer

**Confidence: medium-high.** The reasons are generally well documented, but historical product changes are unevenly documented. Confidence would rise with archived documentation or release notes for Statsig, VWO, Adobe Target, and GrowthBook.

The main stated reasons separate into four themes.

**Avoiding a hidden growth of false positives.** Adobe, Amplitude, Eppo, Statsig, GrowthBook, LinkedIn, Netflix, Microsoft, and Optimizely all explicitly discuss the inflation caused by looking at many hypotheses. Adobe frames this primarily as family-wise false-positive risk; Optimizely instead argued that FDR better matched the "incorrect business decision" risk associated with many variation-goal combinations. **Power versus error control.** Eppo explicitly says wider intervals under preferential Bonferroni reduce false positives at the cost of more false negatives. Amplitude calls Bonferroni conservative. Optimizely's historical motivation for FDR was to lose less power than strict family-wise control while still controlling the proportion of false discoveries. Spotify's current argument is different again: it says much of Bonferroni's conservatism disappears if the family contains only success metrics and excludes guardrails that cannot cause a false-positive shipping decision. **Metric relevance and role.** LinkedIn's expected-versus-unexpected threshold rule and Microsoft's 2026 treatment-effect-assessment article both stress that irrelevant metrics should not be treated as though they carry the same prior or decision relevance as intended outcomes. Optimizely operationalizes this through primary, secondary, and monitoring tiers. Eppo gives extra alpha to the primary metric. Spotify goes furthest by tying the correction family to the logical shipping rule, separating success and guardrail metrics. **Exploration versus confirmation.** LaunchDarkly explicitly says filtered results are for exploration and hypothesis generation because CUPED, MCC, and sequential adjustments are not applied. Amplitude similarly does not correct group-bys. Adobe says post-test segmentation is useful but recommends validating a discovered subgroup in a new experiment or manually applying Bonferroni. Airbnb's post-selection paper formalizes the underlying problem: selecting favorable results among many metrics, segments, or treatments biases effect estimates and interval coverage, not merely p-values. There are also important limits and counterexamples. **Benjamini-Hochberg does not generally provide the same guarantee as FWER control**, so a system advertising FDR is answering a different error-rate question. **Ordinary Bonferroni can be dominated by Holm for FWER testing**, although Bonferroni has practical advantages for precomputing equal marginal thresholds and simultaneous intervals. R's official documentation summarizes the classical result that Holm gives strong FWER control under arbitrary dependence and dominates unmodified Bonferroni. Dependence is also not a purely theoretical concern in these platforms. Microsoft reports strong correlation among production scorecard metrics and says Fisher's method gave dramatically inflated scorecard false-positive rates in an A/A validation, while BH behaved close enough to the intended rate to become its default TEA method. That finding is evidence for Microsoft's particular scorecards, not a theorem that ordinary BH controls FDR under arbitrary dependence. The strongest documented method changes I found are these. Optimizely publicly launched Stats Engine on **January 21, 2015**, replacing classical fixed-horizon Type I error control with sequential testing plus FDR control. GrowthBook's **version 2.1 in 2023** added user-selectable multiple-testing correction, then described Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg; more recent 2026 material refers to Holm-Bonferroni and BH. Spotify Confidence's current public position is Bonferroni with a narrow, role-based family, and its 2026 writing explicitly says Spotify had previously added more correction methods and later removed them. Microsoft ExP's July 15, 2026 article says BH-adjusted scorecard p-values are now reported by default for TEA. For VWO, I found a methodological discrepancy but not enough evidence to call it a dated replacement: its multivariate-testing page says it uses a "latest Sidak correction," while its current SmartStats page says "built-in Bonferroni correction" for multiple variations. These may apply to different test types or generations of the product. For Adobe, the apparent discrepancy is product-specific rather than necessarily temporal: standard Target planning material recommends or offers Bonferroni for multiple offers, while **Analytics for Target (A4T)** explicitly says its confidence calculation does not apply Bonferroni for multiple offers. ## Key-claim evidence
